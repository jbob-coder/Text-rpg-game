## Economic transformation

Artificial portals do not make resources free.

They create a new cost stack:
- portal energy;
- anchor construction;
- calibration;
- security;
- quarantine;
- labor;
- environmental protection;
- return-route redundancy;
- insurance;
- political risk.

A remote deposit is valuable only if its extracted value exceeds the complete corridor cost.

## Early profitable sectors

- rare crystal deposits;
- high-value metals;
- pharmaceuticals/biological compounds;
- research specimens;
- specialized agriculture;
- strategic military resources.

Bulk commodities remain uneconomical until portal capacity improves.

## Frontier labor

Labor systems range from:
- highly paid specialists;
- military assignments;
- voluntary settlers;
- debt contracts;
- prison labor in some jurisdictions;
- illegal coercive labor under some jurisdictions, criminal networks, or abusive contractors;
- corporate indenture-like arrangements.

This variation becomes a major political issue.

## Settlement lifecycle

Typical successful settlement stages:
1. robotic survey;
2. security/science camp;
3. extraction or research outpost;
4. permanent utility core;
5. civilian support population;
6. local agriculture/manufacturing;
7. political identity and demands for autonomy.

Failed settlements may be:
- evacuated;
- abandoned;
- quarantined;
- cut off;
- absorbed by another polity;
- lost after route collapse.

## Earth effect

Portal resources reduce some shortages but increase inequality between:
- states with reliable corridors;
- states without portal access;
- major hub cities;
- disconnected regions.

This inequality helps drive later geopolitical competition and interworld territorial disputes.


## Homunculus clarification

The Homunculus Network is not treated as a labor caste or ordinary economic population in this framework.

Its active canon is a separate hidden hostile network. Portal-frontier labor systems must be modeled independently from Homunculus biology/origin assumptions.
