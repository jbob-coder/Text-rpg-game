## Worker classes

Common roles:
- recovery worker;
- harvest specialist;
- assay technician;
- container inspector;
- resonance engineer;
- refinery operator;
- decontamination technician;
- crystal logistics driver;
- occupational medic;
- utility stabilizer technician;
- certified storage custodian.

## Risk gradient

Lower average exposure:
- administrative/compliance;
- clean assay interpretation;
- planning;
- insured institutional engineering.

Higher average exposure:
- carcass recovery;
- contaminated-site cleanup;
- refinery maintenance;
- waste handling;
- emergency containment;
- illegal extraction.

## Shift design

High-risk operations increasingly use:
- shorter exposure blocks;
- buddy systems;
- post-shift decontamination;
- mandatory screening;
- incident dosimetry/field logs;
- controlled rest periods.

Typical high-risk controlled shift:
4–6 hours direct-zone work inside an 8–12 hour duty period.

Exact limits vary by hazard.

## Worker housing

Industrial districts create nearby worker neighborhoods because:
- controlled gates slow commuting;
- emergency callout requires proximity;
- specialized labor clusters around plants.

Housing quality varies sharply.

Better regulated cities provide:
- clean-water monitoring;
- worker clinics;
- transit;
- buffer distance.

Poor districts may place housing too close to freight and waste sites.

## Labor politics

Major disputes:
- hazard pay;
- right to exposure records;
- medical privacy;
- employer responsibility for latent illness;
- right to refuse unstable lots;
- use of ability users in high-risk roles;
- contractor substitution;
- contamination compensation.

## Cultural effect

Crystal work becomes both prestigious and stigmatized.

Engineers may be treated as elite technical workers.

Recovery and waste crews may be viewed as dirty, dangerous labor despite their importance.
