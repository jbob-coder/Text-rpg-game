The portal age did not begin when people stepped through a doorway.

It began when a machine opened one without waiting for nature.

### 2559–2562 — reproducing the boundary

The Mk3 anchor arrays of 2558 could hold selected natural breaches stable.

That was not enough.

Portal physicists wanted to reproduce the boundary conditions themselves.

The first artificial microbreach experiments were conducted in chambers designed to fail inward:
- massive conventional shielding;
- remotely operated equipment;
- sacrificial sensors;
- pressure isolation;
- no personnel inside the active test volume.

Most early attempts produced nothing.

Some produced unstable field distortion.

Several damaged equipment.

In 2562, one experiment produced a measurable artificial aperture lasting less than a second.

Nothing crossed.

That was enough.

For the first time, a portal event existed because humans had deliberately created the boundary rather than captured a natural one.

### The addressing problem

Opening a hole was not useful if nobody knew where it went.

Researchers therefore treated destination selection as an information problem.

A natural breach had a measurable resonance signature.

If that signature could be stored and recreated, perhaps the artificial aperture could be biased toward the same destination.

The work required years because signatures drifted.

A destination was not a fixed telephone number.

It behaved more like a moving solution requiring:
- field correction;
- time correction;
- environmental calibration;
- repeated sampling.

In 2566, a laboratory reopened an artificial aperture toward a previously sampled destination region.

The aperture was small.

The result was enormous.

A portal could now possess an address.

### 2569 — bringing something back

Sending a probe through was easier than recovering it.

Return required:
- a destination-side beacon;
- power;
- a return signature;
- an Earth-side receiving window;
- timing precise enough that the probe did not become stranded.

The first successful unmanned return mission carried less than the public imagined:
- environmental samples;
- imagery;
- instrument logs;
- damaged equipment.

But its most important cargo was proof.

A controlled round trip was possible.

### Human transit was treated like a launch

By 2572, the first human crossing did not resemble commuting.

It resembled a combined spacecraft launch, hazardous-material operation, military mission, and surgical procedure.

The team trained for:
- pressure failure;
- portal collapse;
- loss of return;
- contamination;
- injury in an alien environment;
- communications loss;
- equipment failure.

Families signed risk documents.

Medical baselines were recorded.

Return quarantine was prepared before departure.

The transit itself was brief.

Preparation took months.

### The first crossing

The first team crossed into a previously sampled region under the operational designation DESTINATION_HAVEN_03.

The name was deliberately optimistic.

The environment was not paradise.

It was merely one of the first destinations where protected human operations were considered survivable.

The team remained close to the portal endpoint.

They did not explore heroically across a continent.

They checked air, soil, microorganisms, line of sight, terrain, structural stability, and return systems.

They came back.

That restraint is why they succeeded.

## What changed

PUBLIC:
- artificial portal generation exists;
- repeatable addressing exists under controlled conditions;
- humans have crossed and returned.

CLASSIFIED:
- destination signatures drift enough to make route maintenance strategically important;
- several apparently promising destinations were rejected for biological or atmospheric reasons;
- transit systems remain vulnerable to cascading power/control failure.

TRUE:
- the portal is becoming infrastructure, but every route is still a technical relationship between two dangerous environments.
