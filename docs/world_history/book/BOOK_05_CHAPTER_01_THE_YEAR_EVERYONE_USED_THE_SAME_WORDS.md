The Crystal Standards Congress of 2506 did not make crystals safer.

It made disagreement measurable.

Before the congress, laboratories could describe the same sample in different language and still believe they agreed. Insurers used their own categories. Military laboratories used classified shorthand. Hospitals cared about biological behavior. Freight authorities cared about whether a container could cross a bridge or pass through a tunnel.

Industrialization began when all of those institutions were forced to describe a sample in terms another institution could understand.

### 2506 — the congress

The congress brought together researchers, regulators, logistics officers, insurers, hospital physicists, military engineers, ecologists, and industrial representatives.

The central argument was whether one crystal should receive one simple grade.

The proposal failed.

A sample could have low useful output and still be biologically dangerous.
Another could produce high industrial output while remaining stable in certified storage.
A beast-derived crystal could be valuable because of bioactivity rather than power density.

The adopted framework therefore separated:
- source;
- stability;
- output;
- bioactivity;
- hazard.

That decision shaped the next century.

### Standardization changed buildings

A standard is not only a document.

It changes architecture.

Assay laboratories needed calibrated rooms.
Freight yards needed inspection lanes.
Warehouses needed storage separated by hazard.
Hospitals needed certified equipment rooms.
Factories needed emergency isolation.
Insurance inspectors needed access.

A sample could now move legally from mine, carcass, laboratory, or breach site through an auditable chain.

The chain was slow.

It was also scalable.

### 2507–2510 — chain of custody

The new legal and scientific language produced a second requirement: provenance.

Governments and insurers wanted to know:
- where a crystal came from;
- who handled it;
- how it had been transported;
- whether it had been exposed to biological material;
- whether its measured behavior had changed.

Chain-of-custody systems became part of ordinary industrial paperwork.

Workers complained about them.
Smugglers learned to forge them.
Courts depended on them.
Insurers demanded them.

By 2510, the first interoperable resonance assay systems made it possible for laboratories in different regions to compare the same categories of response with far greater confidence.

### 2509 — the sealed cell

The first certified crystal cell did not replace the electrical grid.

It solved narrower problems extremely well.

A sealed unit could supply compact, long-duration energy to:
- emergency systems;
- remote monitoring stations;
- medical backup equipment;
- military field devices.

The early cells were expensive.

They required specialist maintenance.

Their output depended on certified feedstock.

A damaged housing could turn a useful device into a hazardous-material incident.

For those reasons, cell rooms were often more important than the cells themselves.

### Labor becomes specialized

Standardization created new workers.

Assay technicians.
Certified container inspectors.
Cell maintenance specialists.
Industrial contamination medics.
Crystal logistics controllers.
Resonance engineers.

Training became credentialed.

A person who had survived Veinfall through experience could no longer automatically qualify for industrial work twenty years later.

The industry was becoming professional.

That created safety.

It also created a divide between licensed knowledge and informal knowledge.

### Ability law enters the industrial workplace

The 2514 public-safety model did not settle ability politics.

It changed the vocabulary.

A worker with an innate ability was not treated simply as a hazardous object.

Employers increasingly had to distinguish:
- the existence of an ability;
- deliberate professional use;
- foreseeable workplace risk;
- negligent use;
- intentional harm.

Some employers adapted jobs around useful abilities.

Others excluded users through risk policy.

Unions and courts began contesting the line.

### Ordinary life by 2518

Most households still did not own crystal power devices.

Most cars, appliances, lights, communications systems, and domestic infrastructure remained conventional.

But citizens increasingly encountered crystal systems indirectly:
- hospital backup power;
- remote sensors;
- emergency communications;
- hazardous-industry tools;
- regulated freight;
- government reserves.

This is how industrial revolutions often begin.

Not with every home transforming at once.

With infrastructure becoming dependent before consumers notice.
