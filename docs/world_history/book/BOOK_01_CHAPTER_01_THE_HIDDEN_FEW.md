## A world that remembered people before it understood powers

The first persistent anomalous lineages did not call themselves anomalous.

They did not know that future researchers would place them inside categories such as PHYSICAL, SENSORY, BIOLOGICAL, ENERGY, MENTAL, SPATIAL, TRANSFORMATION, MATERIAL, FIELD, or ANOMALOUS.

Those classifications belonged to a world with laboratories, measurement standards, genetic databases, field sensors, medical imaging, and centuries of accumulated cases.

The ancient world had none of that.

It had memory.

If a child repeatedly survived injuries that should have killed them, people remembered.

If a hunter could find water with impossible consistency, people remembered.

If one family produced generations of individuals with unusual perception, people remembered.

If someone became dangerous during fear, fever, grief, puberty, childbirth, battle, or ritual stress, people remembered that too.

Memory, however, is not science.

It becomes story.

And stories change.

## Settlement changed the problem

Permanent settlements created something mobile human populations had rarely possessed at the same scale: continuity of observation.

A community might now remember three generations of the same family.

Marriage could be tracked.

Inheritance mattered.

Land and status could pass between relatives.

Ritual roles could become hereditary.

A village might notice that one household produced healers more often than others, even if most members of that household were ordinary.

A ruling family might cultivate a reputation for divine favor because one ancestor had displayed an ability no one could explain.

A persecuted lineage might deliberately marry outward, change names, migrate, or suppress its own oral history.

This is the period in which rare ability expression becomes entangled with social structure.

Not because humanity suddenly understood the phenomenon.

Because humans became better at remembering kin.

## The statistical cruelty of rarity

The phenomenon remained rare enough to resist general recognition.

Even much later, before Veinfall, the broad estimated rate of obvious manifested users averaged roughly one person per five to twenty million humans.

Ancient populations were smaller, unevenly distributed, and frequently disrupted.

A powerful lineage could vanish in a famine.

A child with unusual traits could die of infection before anyone understood what made them different.

A family could carry latent compatibility for centuries without producing an obvious user.

A manifestation could be so subtle that it became known only as talent, intuition, unusual health, charisma, luck, sensitivity, or superstition.

At the opposite extreme, a dramatic manifestation could destroy the user's safety.

Rarity protected the secret and endangered the individual.

## The many meanings of one impossible event

There was never one ancient response to ability users.

Culture mattered.

Religion mattered.

Politics mattered.

The nature of the manifestation mattered.

A person who could endure cold might be useful.

A person who could produce visible heat might be sacred in one place and terrifying in another.

A child who heard things no one else heard might be considered gifted, cursed, ill, chosen, deceptive, or dangerous depending on local belief.

A warrior with impossible reflexes might become a legend without anyone recognizing a biological anomaly.

A healer might gain followers.

A healer whose failures were noticed could just as easily become a target.

The world did not divide neatly into enlightened protectors and ignorant persecutors.

Communities made decisions based on fear, survival, belief, ambition, family interest, and power.

Some protected unusual people.

Some exploited them.

Some worshiped them.

Some hid them.

Some killed them.

Many simply never realized what they were seeing.

## The birth of distorted legends

From approximately 6,000 BCE onward, written civilizations began preserving more information, but writing did not solve the interpretation problem.

Records described events through the concepts available to the writer.

A ruler's impossible victory could become proof of divine favor.

A person with a transformation-type manifestation could become the seed of a monster tradition.

An individual whose ability affected perception might be remembered as an oracle.

A biological ability could become a miracle story.

A spatial anomaly could be recorded as sacred ground, curse, doorway, haunting, or forbidden region.

But most myths were still myths.

This distinction is essential.

The existence of real anomalous humans does not make ancient religion, folklore, or legendary creatures automatically factual.

Later historians repeatedly damaged serious research by treating every supernatural story as hidden evidence.

The reliable pattern is smaller and more frustrating: a minority of old accounts contain fragments of real anomalous events mixed with cultural interpretation, exaggeration, political propaganda, symbolic storytelling, translation error, and later invention.

The task of reconstruction is not to believe the legend.

It is to identify the residue that legend may have preserved.

## Lineages learned concealment before institutions learned detection

Some families discovered, over generations, that visibility was dangerous.

They did not understand genetics.

They understood consequences.

A family known for strange children might be excluded from marriage.

A ruler might demand service.

A priesthood might claim ownership over the family's role.

A military leader might keep gifted relatives close.

A rival might accuse them publicly.

As states became larger, unusual people became potentially useful to organized power.

That did not create one global secret organization.

It created local patterns.

A royal court might quietly retain someone with unusual senses.

A religious order might protect people believed to possess visions.

A criminal network might exploit a person with an ability useful for theft, intimidation, transport, or information.

A family might build rules around never demonstrating its talent in public.

Another might intentionally mythologize itself to increase political status.

Across centuries, the same underlying phenomenon could therefore produce opposite social outcomes.

## The problem of inheritance

Ancient observers who noticed family clustering often assumed the ability itself passed directly through blood.

Modern science considers that too simple.

The heritable component appears to affect compatibility and probability, not guarantee an exact manifestation.

Two related users may possess very different effects.

A family may have several generations with no obvious manifestation.

A child may be the first visible user in recorded family memory.

A lineage famous for one kind of ability may later produce something that appears unrelated.

This unpredictability prevented even sophisticated ancient observers from building a stable breeding model.

It also helped the phenomenon disappear into ordinary human variation.

Families could preserve stories without possessing control.

## Early power structures around the gifted

As kingdoms, empires, religious institutions, and long-distance trade networks developed, anomalous individuals occasionally entered systems larger than their home communities.

Some became servants of rulers.

Some became advisors.

Some were hidden in religious communities.

Some were used in war.

Some were imprisoned.

Some escaped into places where state authority was weaker.

None of these practices were globally coordinated.

Records were often deliberately obscured because an unusual person could be politically valuable.

A ruler had little incentive to publish that a trusted scout, bodyguard, healer, negotiator, or interrogator possessed an inexplicable advantage.

This created one of the oldest recurring patterns in anomalous history:

the more strategically useful the individual, the less reliable the public record tended to become.

## Skepticism was often rational

It would be easy, from the perspective of 2670, to imagine that ancient people constantly ignored obvious superhuman evidence.

The truth is less simple.

Fraud existed.

Rumor existed.

Political mythology existed.

Mistaken diagnosis existed.

Religious performance existed.

Coincidence existed.

Exaggeration existed.

A society that believed every claim of impossible ability would be easy to manipulate.

Skepticism protected people from deception even while it sometimes concealed real cases.

The ancient world therefore developed a paradox that lasted until Veinfall:

real abilities existed inside a much larger ocean of false, symbolic, mistaken, or unverifiable supernatural claims.

That made the truth exceptionally hard to isolate.

## From miracle to anomaly

By the late medieval and early modern periods, some investigators began recording unusual human phenomena with greater attention to repeatability, witness agreement, anatomy, and physical consequence.

They still lacked the tools to identify the underlying mechanism.

But the language slowly changed.

A miracle was a theological category.

A curse was a moral or spiritual category.

A prodigy was a social category.

An anomaly was the beginning of a scientific one.

This transition did not happen everywhere at once, and it did not eliminate religious or cultural interpretations.

It simply created a new way to ask the question:

What if the impossible event happens under conditions that can be observed again?

That question would eventually become dangerous.

## What ordinary people experienced

For nearly all human beings living in this period, none of this formed a hidden global drama.

Most people never knowingly met a manifested ability user.

Their lives were shaped by ordinary pressures:
- harvest;
- weather;
- disease;
- childbirth;
- taxation;
- war;
- trade;
- family obligation;
- religion;
- local law;
- migration;
- famine;
- craft;
- status.

A real ability user might pass through a town and never be recognized.

A family might hide one child.

A ruler might protect one servant.

A monastery, shrine, court, military household, or merchant network might preserve one strange record.

Then decades or centuries could pass.

This is how a real phenomenon remained historically discontinuous.

## What the old world did not have

Before the modern scientific period, humanity still lacked:
- a global dataset;
- standardized ability categories;
- reliable physiological testing;
- a concept of crystal-induced capability;
- stable portal science;
- a beast taxonomy;
- dedicated ability medicine;
- laws written around manifested powers as a recognized biological reality;
- schools designed to train ability users systematically.

The Academy of the present era is therefore not an ancient institution unchanged across time.

Its distant ancestors, when they eventually emerge, will come from the gradual convergence of military training, scientific study, public safety, medicine, and later beast/portal response.

## PUBLIC, CLASSIFIED, and TRUE

### PUBLIC understanding in the period

There is no single public understanding.

Different cultures interpret unusual events through local belief, politics, medicine, rumor, law, and religion.

No civilization possesses a confirmed global model of natural human abilities.

### CLASSIFIED / restricted understanding

Some courts, orders, families, military institutions, and later state systems maintain private records.

Their knowledge is local and fragmented.

No one possesses the full phenomenon.

### TRUE author-history

- some natural ability users are real;
- recurrent lineages exist;
- most alleged supernatural claims are not evidence of real abilities;
- no single organization controls the phenomenon;
- the exact origin of natural abilities remains unresolved;
- crystal civilization has not yet begun.

## End condition

By 1600 CE, humanity has accumulated:
- thousands of years of distorted stories;
- scattered family traditions;
- religious and political records;
- occasional concealed state knowledge;
- no unified theory.

The phenomenon remains hidden not because everyone agreed to hide it, but because no one had enough trustworthy pieces to prove what the pieces meant.

The next historical transformation is methodological.

Science begins to create tools capable of separating rare genuine anomalies from the much larger body of rumor.

And once governments learn that even a few cases are real, secrecy stops being accidental.

It becomes policy.
