The first days of Veinfall taught humanity to fear places.

The next days taught it to fear change itself.

At first, responders expected the biological crisis to resemble contamination: identify the agent, isolate the exposed, disinfect surfaces, protect food and water, and wait for the source to decay.

That assumption failed because there was no single agent.

The same global disturbance that changed mineral structure and opened unstable breaches was also changing the conditions under which living systems functioned. The effect differed from valley to valley, from species to species, and sometimes from one individual organism to another.

Most exposed life did not become stronger.

A great deal of it died.

### Day 3 — mortality before adaptation

In high-exposure pockets, insects fell from walls and vegetation in numbers large enough to clog drainage grates. Fish surfaced in shallow water where dissolved oxygen and local chemistry shifted. Small mammals abandoned nests in daylight. Birds changed migration direction without an obvious weather cause.

Veterinary and agricultural teams initially treated the pattern as poisoning.

They were partly correct.

Some deaths were caused by toxic mineral release, some by temperature change, some by disrupted metabolism, some by tissue crystallization, some by panic and stampede, and some by causes that could not yet be measured.

The crucial discovery was that survival was selective.

Two animals exposed at the same distance from the same anomaly did not always respond the same way.

One might die within hours.

Another might recover.

A third might survive with altered tissue, sensory behavior, or metabolism.

That variance became the seed of an entirely new biological science.

### Plants did not stay passive

The public imagination later focused on animals because animals moved, attacked, fled, and could be filmed.

Plants changed more quietly.

Near several high-exposure zones, roots developed hard translucent inclusions. Some trees sealed damaged vascular tissue around mineral growth rather than dying immediately. Grasses in contaminated soil showed patchy color loss followed by unexpectedly rapid regrowth. In other areas, whole stands collapsed because crystal-bearing deposits interfered with water transport.

The first responders called some of these effects "glass rot."

The term was scientifically useless, but it spread because people needed names before they had explanations.

Agricultural agencies faced an immediate problem: a crop could look intact while its roots, soil microbiome, or stored grain had already changed.

Food inspection therefore became a crisis discipline almost overnight.

### Fungi and microorganisms moved faster than policy

Fungal mats appeared in damp exclusion zones where ordinary competitors had died back.

Some were terrestrial organisms exploiting damaged ecosystems.

Others were altered forms.

A few samples recovered near breaches did not match known terrestrial lineages closely enough for confident identification.

Bacteria changed even faster.

Hospitals and laboratories discovered that "sterile" was becoming a temporary condition in some anomaly zones. Biofilms formed on surfaces that had passed ordinary cleaning protocols. Water plants recorded microbial blooms with unusual mineral content.

This did not mean every new colony was dangerous.

The danger was uncertainty.

An emergency system built around known pathogens suddenly had to manage organisms that might be harmless, toxic, symbiotic, invasive, or dependent on a field condition no one understood.

### The first hostile crystal-bearing fauna

The first confirmed incidents did not resemble the giant monsters of later military doctrine.

They were smaller, confused, injured, and unpredictable.

At Red Basin, a small group of native canids that had survived exposure developed rigid subdermal mineral plaques around the shoulders and rib line. Two could still move normally. One exhibited severe tremors. Another attacked vehicles and responders after repeated attempts to drive it away from a water source.

When one animal was killed, field staff found a dense crystal-associated structure near the sternum.

No one knew whether the structure had caused the aggression, enabled survival, or was simply another symptom.

The incident became important because it combined four things in one animal:
- survival after severe exposure;
- persistent anatomical change;
- abnormal behavior;
- a recoverable crystal-associated structure.

Responders did not yet possess a mature category for such an organism.

The later word "beast" was still only crisis slang.

### Breach organisms

At the East Harbor urban breach, small segmented organisms crossed during a brief stabilization interval.

Most died within minutes.

A few survived long enough to move into drainage channels and service voids beneath damaged buildings.

They were not enormous.

They were dangerous because nobody knew:
- whether they were venomous;
- whether they could reproduce;
- whether they carried microorganisms;
- what they ate;
- whether they were juvenile forms of something larger.

The first containment teams learned that an unknown organism small enough to disappear into a wall could create more operational difficulty than an animal large enough to shoot.

### Day 5 to Day 7 — the ecology stops being local

By the middle of the primary cascade, ecological effects began interacting with human evacuation.

Wild animals entered roads and settlements because established territories had become hostile.

Livestock transport increased disease risk.

Displaced people carried seeds, insects, pets, contaminated clothing, soil, water, and equipment across emergency boundaries.

Emergency food distribution concentrated humans and animals in the same logistical corridors.

A quarantine line designed for a portal could be bypassed by a truck that had driven through contaminated mud twenty kilometers away.

The crisis was no longer a collection of isolated zones.

It was becoming a network.

### Six regions, six different disasters

At Red Basin, distance was an advantage. Authorities could close roads kilometers from the most dangerous terrain. The cost was isolation: ranches, field teams, and small communities beyond checkpoints could wait hours for medical support.

At Greenvale, wildlife density made simple exclusion impossible. Fencing protected people but redirected animals into farms and secondary roads. Veterinary teams became as important as police.

At the Central Asia corridor, the geometry of evacuation was linear. Roads, rail approaches, and mountain passes became chokepoints. A safe valley could become inaccessible because one bridge or tunnel sat inside a hazard envelope.

At East Harbor, density turned every radius into a compromise. People lived above and below one another. Closing one street displaced thousands into the next. Vertical evacuation, ventilation control, and building access mattered more than drawing circles on maps.

In the North Atlantic, the crisis was maritime. Ships could move away, but ocean currents could not be fenced. Samples collected hundreds of kilometers apart might belong to the same moving contamination field.

In the South Pacific, pressure anomalies and remote distance meant that the first line of defense was navigation control. A site with almost no civilians could still threaten shipping, fisheries, and weather monitoring over a vast area.

Veinfall was global.

Its biology was local.

### Day 8 and Day 9 — the first ecological triage

By Day 8, authorities in the best-organized regions stopped asking whether they could preserve every pre-Veinfall condition.

They could not.

The question became what had to be saved first.

Drinking-water systems.

Seed reserves.

Livestock breeding populations.

Hospitals.

Wastewater treatment.

Pollinator zones.

Transport corridors.

Research samples.

Food warehouses.

Ecological triage was a grim concept: accept loss in one place to prevent cascading failure somewhere else.

That principle would later shape exclusion zones, beast preserves, controlled harvests, and the first professional crystal-ecology institutions.

But during Veinfall, it was improvisation.

People were learning which pieces of an ecosystem could fail before a city failed with them.

## What changed by chapter end

PUBLIC:
- strange animal behavior and visible biological changes can no longer be dismissed as isolated contamination;
- videos of altered fauna circulate widely;
- food and water safety become central fears;
- the word "beast" begins spreading as a crisis term.

CLASSIFIED:
- stable crystal-associated structures have been recovered from some surviving animals;
- breach-transferred organisms can survive Earth conditions for nontrivial periods;
- some plant, fungal, and microbial changes persist after local field intensity declines.

TRUE:
- multiple ecological mechanisms are operating at once;
- most exposure is harmful or lethal;
- rare survivors are beginning the pathways that will later produce stable crystal-adapted lineages;
- cross-world ecology has begun before humanity has any controlled portal-travel capability.

## Protagonist mystery boundary

This chapter establishes no connection between Veinfall biology and the unique origin of Jack Wilson's Steal or Elias Voss's Perfect Clone.

Those remain unresolved.
