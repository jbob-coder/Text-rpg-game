The first generation of Crystal Era technology did not enter homes as miracle machines.

It entered as supervised equipment.

Hospitals received fixed diagnostic units.
Industrial sites received controlled thermal modules.
Emergency services received better field detectors.
Laboratories received stronger storage.
Cities received rules for moving dangerous material through ordinary streets.

This was the period when humanity learned that a technology could be useful without yet being ordinary.

### The problem was trust

By 2489, researchers could demonstrate that some crystal samples produced repeatable effects.

That did not mean two cities agreed on what "safe" meant.

Regional certification systems had evolved independently.

One authority measured storage stability over thirty days.
Another required ninety.
One allowed supervised hospital use.
Another prohibited any civilian building from housing an active sample.

Insurers responded by creating their own categories.

A building owner might legally possess a crystal device and still be unable to insure the property.

A transport company might be licensed to move one class of sample but denied access to a tunnel or dense residential district.

The first civilian crystal economy therefore grew inside paperwork.

### Fixed systems came before portable freedom

Portable crystal technology remained difficult because movement created risk.

A fixed unit could be:
- shielded;
- monitored;
- bolted to a foundation;
- isolated behind walls;
- supplied with emergency ventilation;
- inspected regularly.

The earliest civilian applications were therefore large enough to have rooms built around them.

Hospitals used crystal-assisted sensing rigs under specialist supervision.

Industrial plants experimented with controlled heat and field-response systems.

Research facilities used high-density storage for emergency backup.

Hazard teams used more sensitive detectors.

None of these systems resembled the compact, standardized crystal cells that would appear after 2506.

The infrastructure came first.

Miniaturization came later.

### The first legal markets

Governments faced a contradiction.

They wanted crystal research and economic growth.

They also knew that uncontrolled sale had already produced deaths, fraud, theft, and black-market experimentation.

The compromise was the licensed regional market.

Approved sellers could transfer approved materials to approved buyers under documented conditions.

The documents mattered as much as the crystal.

A legal shipment needed:
- declared origin;
- sample weight;
- handling profile;
- transport container;
- route authorization;
- buyer license;
- storage destination;
- inspection seal.

There was still no globally interoperable standard.

That would not arrive until the 2506 Crystal Standards Congress.

The 2489–2505 market was therefore regional, fragmented, and expensive.

### Smuggling became more sophisticated

Early smugglers had carried fragments in improvised containers.

By the 2490s, professional networks learned to exploit gaps between jurisdictions.

A sample prohibited in one state could be legal under research exemption in another.

A licensed biological shipment could conceal illicit crystal tissue.

A decommissioned exclusion route could still provide access to deposits that official maps considered closed.

Smuggling shifted from scavenging to logistics.

The criminal advantage was not only secrecy.

It was knowing which paperwork system disagreed with which other paperwork system.

### Protecting beasts from people

The first response to dangerous altered fauna had often been eradication.

By the 2490s, ecologists had evidence that this could produce worse outcomes.

Removing a crystal-adapted predator could cause prey populations to expand.
Destroying scavengers could leave contaminated carcasses in the environment.
Clearing one nesting area could shift a migration route through a town.

Protected anomaly habitats therefore appeared for reasons that were ecological, scientific, and economic.

At the same time, certain organisms contained valuable crystal structures.

That created pressure for legal harvest.

The compromise was limited research harvest:
- quotas;
- scientific permits;
- carcass reporting;
- protected breeding zones;
- seasonal closures.

This was not yet the mature commercial harvest system of later decades.

But it established the central conflict:

A beast could be dangerous, valuable, and ecologically necessary at the same time.

### A new kind of case law

Courts began receiving cases that pre-Veinfall law had never anticipated.

Could someone be guilty merely for possessing an innate ability?

What counted as negligence when an ability activated during panic?

Could an employer reject a worker whose ability created a documented industrial hazard?

Could police use specialized restraints before a person had committed violence?

Could a school compel medical disclosure?

Over time, several durable legal principles emerged in some jurisdictions.

Possession of an innate ability was not treated as possession of contraband.

Intent, recklessness, and foreseeability mattered.

Involuntary manifestation was not automatically criminal, though resulting harm could still create liability.

Professional use could require training and licensing.

These principles were uneven and contested.

They were also foundational.

### Schools changed before academies did

Public education had already adopted manifestation drills.

By the 2490s, schools began teaching:
- crystal hazard symbols;
- exclusion-zone etiquette;
- what to do during an involuntary manifestation;
- why unknown organisms should not be approached;
- how to report suspicious crystal material;
- basic portal/breach shelter procedure.

Specialist colleges went further.

Field-response schools began sharing instructors.
Medical programs trained alongside hazard units.
Military engineering schools sent students to ecological courses.
Ability-response doctrine entered police and emergency academies.

The institutional walls were beginning to weaken.

That convergence would eventually matter more than any single school name.
