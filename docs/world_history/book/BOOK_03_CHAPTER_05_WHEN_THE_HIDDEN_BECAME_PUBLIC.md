Human abilities did not begin during Veinfall.

Public certainty did.

For thousands of years, unusual humans could disappear into rumor, sealed files, family silence, religious language, classified medicine, or simple disbelief.

Veinfall destroyed that scale advantage.

### The hospitals noticed first

Emergency departments were already overloaded by crush injuries, exposure sickness, panic attacks, burns, respiratory problems, contaminated water, and ordinary accidents caused by failing infrastructure.

Then patients began doing things the medical system could not file under ordinary trauma.

A teenager under extreme stress bent a steel bed rail without the leverage required to do it.

A feverish patient described people moving in a corridor he could not physically see.

A paramedic produced a localized thermal discharge after touching a damaged crystal-bearing surface.

Another patient suffered violent muscular contraction and organ failure while emitting no visible effect at all.

The problem was not simply "people have powers."

The problem was classification.

Was a manifestation:
- a natural ability that had existed latently;
- a new injury;
- crystal contamination;
- a neurological crisis;
- a temporary field effect;
- an induced capability;
- fraud;
- mass panic;
- or some combination?

No hospital had a reliable answer.

### Families were forced to choose

For families with old secrets, Veinfall created a different crisis.

Some lineages already knew that unusual traits existed.

Their survival strategy had often been silence.

Now a child could manifest in a shelter full of cameras.

A parent could use an ability to save someone and become identifiable forever.

A family that had hidden for generations could be exposed by one emergency.

Some chose secrecy even when disclosure might have improved medical care.

Others disclosed immediately because the old rules no longer mattered.

Some abandoned relatives out of fear.

Others protected them from authorities.

There was no universal response.

### Police and soldiers had no common doctrine

A responder confronting an uncontrolled ability user faced an impossible uncertainty.

The person might be:
- injured;
- terrified;
- dangerous;
- unconscious of what they were doing;
- deliberately violent;
- trying to help;
- unable to stop.

Conventional arrest doctrine assumed that force capability was visible or inferable.

Veinfall broke that assumption.

A frightened unarmed civilian could suddenly become more dangerous than a conventional weapon.

A visibly powerful person could also be completely harmless if left alone.

Several early tragedies came from treating ability manifestation as intentional aggression.

Others came from underestimating real threats.

Those contradictory failures later drove formal ability-response doctrine.

### The secrecy collapse

Government secrecy did not end because governments voluntarily revealed everything they knew.

It ended because too many independent witnesses recorded too many events.

Shelter cameras.

Traffic systems.

Hospital recordings.

Emergency body cameras.

Civilian feeds.

Security footage.

Military leaks.

Private messages copied faster than censors could contain them.

By Day 6, the argument had changed.

Public debate was no longer primarily about whether extraordinary human abilities existed.

It was about what they were, how long authorities had known, and what governments intended to do about them.

### Old programs became liabilities

Institutions that had spent decades or centuries managing anomalous cases in secrecy now faced exposure from two directions.

The public wanted answers.

Governments wanted usable expertise.

Old files suddenly mattered because they contained data on:
- manifestation triggers;
- family clustering;
- medical complications;
- dangerous ability patterns;
- historical cases;
- failed containment;
- known lineages.

At the same time, admitting those files existed could prove that authorities had concealed evidence for generations.

The result was selective disclosure.

Some records were released.

Some were reclassified.

Some disappeared.

Some were copied before destruction.

The modern politics of ability registration began in that scramble.

### The first provisional categories

During Days 5–9, hospitals and security agencies developed crude working labels.

They were inconsistent across regions.

Common emergency distinctions included:
- physical enhancement;
- sensory anomaly;
- biological change;
- emitted energy;
- mental/perceptual effect;
- spatial effect;
- body transformation;
- matter interaction;
- field effect;
- unknown.

These were not yet the mature categories used by later science.

But they created a shared language.

A shared language made regulation possible.

### Fear, heroism, exploitation

Ability users became visible in every social role.

Some rescued trapped civilians.

Some stabilized collapsing structures.

Some stole supplies.

Some panicked and injured people.

Some were attacked by frightened crowds.

Some were recruited by military and emergency units within days.

Some were detained "for observation."

Some disappeared into black-market medical networks.

Some became public symbols before anyone understood what had happened to them.

The same event could make one ability user a hero in one city and a security detainee in another.

### Day 10 — the first political split

By Day 10, governments were already diverging.

Some announced temporary protection against vigilante violence.

Some ordered mandatory reporting of dangerous manifestations.

Some required hospitals to transmit anomaly cases to security services.

Some prohibited detention without an overt public-safety basis.

Some used emergency powers to create broad registration systems.

Others could barely maintain ordinary policing.

This uneven beginning matters because later ability law did not descend from one global decision.

It grew out of different emergency choices made under fear.

### What changed by Day 11

Before Veinfall, a government could plausibly deny that a persistent human ability phenomenon existed.

After Veinfall, that position became impossible.

The question was no longer whether unusual humans were real.

The question was what kind of society would be built around them.

## What changed by chapter end

PUBLIC:
- human abilities are an undeniable worldwide reality;
- public suspicion grows that some institutions knew more before Veinfall than they admitted;
- terminology is unstable but broad manifestation categories begin circulating.

CLASSIFIED:
- pre-Veinfall anomaly archives become strategically valuable;
- lineage information becomes a security and medical resource;
- authorities distinguish some natural manifestations from direct crystal-exposure pathology with limited confidence.

TRUE:
- natural ability architecture predates Veinfall;
- the surge reflects increased manifestation and visibility, not the creation of the phenomenon;
- some Veinfall-related effects are genuinely induced and must not be conflated with natural abilities.
