A frontier stops being temporary long before officials admit it.

The first sign was not a flag.

It was a school timetable.

### The people who were not going home

In 2606, many offworld contracts still described workers as temporary personnel.

Some had already lived beyond Earth for ten years.

Their children knew portal procedures better than old Earth geography.

The legal fiction became harder to maintain.

A settlement needed different institutions when people:
- gave birth there;
- buried relatives there;
- opened shops;
- changed employers without leaving;
- elected local councils;
- argued over property;
- expected their children to graduate there.

The frontier was becoming ordinary life.

### Haven_03 grows outward

Gateward, once a small charter settlement, expanded around a protected portal district.

Housing moved farther from the aperture.

Workshops clustered near freight routes.

Schools and clinics moved into quieter blocks.

Water reserves increased because the settlement no longer assumed that an Earth route would reopen within hours.

The settlement still depended on Earth.

It no longer depended on Earth every day.

That distinction produced political confidence.

### New worlds were chosen for different reasons

Portal science had matured enough that expansion was no longer limited to one successful destination.

Human planners began separating worlds by economic purpose.

CINDER_05 attracted extraction companies because high-value deposits justified expensive transit.

VERDANT_11 attracted agricultural and biological research because controlled zones could support food production and medical study.

Neither was treated as "another Earth."

Every destination required its own:
- quarantine profile;
- atmospheric rules;
- construction methods;
- environmental law;
- emergency reserve policy.

Expansion became specialization.

### The corridor economy

A portal route did not eliminate transport economics.

It concentrated them.

The width of an aperture limited cargo geometry.

Inspection limited throughput.

Quarantine delayed people.

Power maintenance created scheduled downtime.

A mine could have enormous reserves and still fail financially if its corridor could not move enough certified cargo.

This made hub capacity politically important.

A settlement with a reliable P4 corridor could negotiate.

A settlement dependent on one unstable P3 route could not.

### Local government becomes harder to ignore

Frontier councils first handled practical matters:
- water;
- waste;
- school maintenance;
- local roads;
- market permits.

Then they began arguing about:
- resource royalties;
- policing;
- environmental limits;
- Earth-appointed administrators;
- local courts;
- representation.

Earth governments responded differently.

Some decentralized authority.

Some tightened control.

Some treated settlement councils as partners.

Others treated them as contractors who had become inconveniently political.

### The first-contact problem before first contact

Human expansion also created a new anxiety.

What would count as evidence that a destination already belonged to someone?

A straight line in terrain was not enough.

An unusual radio signal was not enough.

A repeating geometric structure might still be natural.

Scientists and governments therefore established evidence thresholds before a confirmed intelligent civilization was encountered.

This mattered because a false claim could stop a valuable expansion project.

A false denial could become something much worse.

### The spaces between worlds

By 2620, the human portal network was no longer one frontier.

It was a hierarchy.

Some routes carried families.

Some carried ore.

Some carried biological samples.

Some remained classified.

Some were closed.

Every route had a political meaning because every route connected two legal spaces.

The door was no longer merely a border.

The network had become geography.
