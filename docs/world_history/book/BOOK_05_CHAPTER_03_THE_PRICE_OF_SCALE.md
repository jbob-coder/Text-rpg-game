The Crystal Era had spent decades proving that extraordinary materials could be measured, insured, transported, and used.

The next problem was what happened when everybody wanted them.

### 2530 — crystals become prices

The first large interoperable commodity exchanges did not trade "power."

They traded certified lots with known:
- source;
- stability;
- output profile;
- bioactivity;
- hazard;
- origin history;
- legal status.

Price discovery changed politics.

A regional shortage was no longer an obscure technical problem.

It appeared on contracts.

It affected utility budgets.

It changed hospital procurement.

It altered military planning.

Crystal-rich regions gained leverage.

Crystal-poor regions gained anxiety.

### The geography of inequality

Industrial regions near deposits, harvest zones, assay centers, and freight corridors became wealthier.

Other regions became dependent on imports.

A crystal-poor state could respond through:
- recycling;
- substitutes;
- efficiency;
- long contracts;
- stockpiles;
- foreign investment;
- aggressive portal research.

A crystal-rich state could become prosperous.

It could also become a target for espionage, corruption, external political pressure, and environmental exploitation.

The resource curse had found a new material.

### 2536 — armor changes procurement

Crystal-composite armor entered military procurement as a specialist system.

It was not invulnerable.

It was not cheap.

Its value came from achieving protection characteristics that ordinary materials could not match at the same mass in selected threat profiles.

That advantage created new problems.

Maintenance mattered.

A damaged plate could fail unpredictably.

Field units needed certified technicians.

Supply depots needed protected storage.

Armor became part of logistics rather than simply equipment.

### 2538 — the harvest collapse

The industrial world had spent years treating crystal-bearing fauna as renewable in theory.

Then one commercially valuable regional population collapsed.

The cause was not one hunt.

It was accumulation:
- quota fraud;
- poaching;
- habitat fragmentation;
- removal of breeding adults;
- demand pressure;
- poor population estimates.

The crisis forced governments to admit that a beast population could be economically valuable precisely because it remained alive.

For the first time, resource planning treated some dangerous fauna like strategic fisheries or forests.

### 2542 — the ethical success

Adaptive prosthetic interfaces changed the tone of public debate.

A patient who had lost a limb or suffered nerve damage could receive a system that used controlled crystal-responsive material to improve feedback and adaptation.

The technology did not erase disability.

It demonstrated that long-term integration between human tissue and crystal-compatible systems could be beneficial rather than pathological.

That success became an argument.

If controlled integration could restore function, what else should medicine be allowed to attempt?

### Medicine becomes enhancement

The line between treatment and enhancement blurred quickly.

A prosthetic restoring normal function was broadly accepted.

A device improving endurance beyond ordinary human limits was more controversial.

A temporary interface that increased sensory performance could be medicine, occupational equipment, military enhancement, or unfair advantage depending on context.

By the mid-2540s, law was chasing technology.

### The first augmentation casualties

Black-market clinics promised what regulated medicine would not.

They used:
- uncertified crystal interfaces;
- altered beast tissue;
- poorly matched implants;
- illegal stimulatory exposure;
- stolen medical hardware.

Some clients improved temporarily.

Others suffered:
- rejection;
- neurological damage;
- crystal growth;
- chronic pain;
- dependence;
- organ injury;
- death.

The existence of failures did not eliminate demand.

It made safe augmentation more politically valuable and unsafe augmentation more criminally profitable.

### Industrial cities become strategic systems

By 2547, a major crystal city was not simply a place with factories.

It contained an ecosystem of:
- refineries;
- assay centers;
- exchanges;
- hospitals;
- defense contractors;
- worker housing;
- reserve depots;
- research campuses;
- freight corridors;
- waste sites.

Destroying or disrupting one part could affect the rest.

Crystal industry had become infrastructure.

Infrastructure had become strategy.
