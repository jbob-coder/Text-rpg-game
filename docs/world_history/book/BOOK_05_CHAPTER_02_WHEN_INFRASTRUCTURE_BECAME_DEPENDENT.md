In 2519, a public electrical network contracted crystal stabilization as a normal service.

The phrase sounds modest.

Its consequence was not.

For the first time, a city designed part of an essential public utility around crystal supply.

### Stabilization was not generation

The early grid systems did not run cities from crystal alone.

They used conventional generation.

Crystal stabilizers helped absorb or smooth specific load conditions where their response profile provided an advantage.

That distinction mattered.

If the crystal system failed, the grid did not become magically powerless.

But the grid could become less stable under peak conditions.

Utilities therefore built redundancy.

Crystal infrastructure entered civilization as one layer among many.

### The first dependency problem

Once a city budgeted around a certified crystal service, the supply chain became strategic.

A delayed shipment could affect maintenance.
A contaminated lot could trigger an inspection shutdown.
A labor strike at a refinery could become an electrical planning problem.
A harvest failure could affect medical or industrial procurement.

Resource policy began moving from laboratory administration toward national economic planning.

### 2522 — medicine earns public trust

The broad certification of a biocrystal diagnostic platform changed public perception.

Medical use had existed earlier under narrow supervision.

The difference in 2522 was reproducibility across accredited hospitals.

Patients could encounter a crystal-assisted diagnostic system without entering an experimental program.

The equipment remained expensive.

It did not cure every crystal-associated disease.

It did something socially important:

It made crystal technology visible as care rather than only danger.

### The harvesting economy

By the mid-2520s, some biological crystals had repeatable commercial value.

That created pressure to move from opportunistic recovery to organized extraction.

The 2526 extraction standard defined:
- safe carcass approach;
- contamination control;
- extraction window;
- tissue preservation;
- chain of custody;
- quality-loss reporting.

The following year, representative harvest licensing systems began regulating species, season, quota, habitat, transport, and disposal.

The industry acquired a new vocabulary.

A "monster carcass" became:
- an ecological record;
- a hazardous biological object;
- a licensed material source;
- an insured asset;
- evidence.

### Workers paid the hidden price

Industrial growth did not distribute risk evenly.

Assay engineers worked in controlled laboratories.

Recovery crews worked in mud, heat, cold, contaminated water, and unstable habitats.

Refinery workers handled repeated low-level exposure.

Drivers carried regulated loads through ordinary communities.

Medical technicians worked with bioactive material around sick patients.

The most advanced institutions developed monitoring and exposure limits.

Poorer contractors sometimes cut corners.

Industrialization created a familiar social pattern:

The people furthest from the profit often stood closest to the hazard.

### The waste problem

Every industry produces something it does not want.

Crystal industry produced:
- contaminated packaging;
- damaged containment;
- unusable fragments;
- biological remains;
- solvent/cleaning waste;
- rejected feedstock;
- low-grade reactive residues.

Early policy had focused on keeping dangerous material inside containers.

Now the question became where the containers went after use.

Disposal sites became politically contentious.

Communities near them demanded monitoring.
Companies argued that overly strict rules would make legal industry uncompetitive.
Criminal networks offered cheaper disposal.

Some of the first long-term industrial contamination scandals began here.

### Cities physically changed

By 2528, a crystal-industrial district had recognizable architecture:
- assay laboratories;
- certified storage;
- freight inspection;
- controlled processing;
- worker clinics;
- decontamination halls;
- emergency isolation yards;
- dedicated utility links;
- wider setbacks between high-risk structures and housing.

Worker districts grew nearby because long commutes through controlled freight zones were expensive and unreliable.

Those neighborhoods gained:
- schools;
- markets;
- union offices;
- clinics;
- bars;
- transport depots.

Industrialization became culture.

### End of the first industrial phase

By 2528, humanity had not yet reached the large commodity exchange of 2530.

But the prerequisites existed.

Materials could be:
- classified;
- assayed;
- insured;
- transported;
- stored;
- medically certified;
- harvested under formal protocol.

The next step was scale.

And scale would introduce overharvest, geopolitical dependency, augmentation, and increasingly aggressive portal engineering.
